**Project:** Information Architecture of Customer Collaboration Portal <br>
**Client:** FDA, Centers for Devices and Radiological Health <br>
**Role:** UX Design Lead

**The tldr;** <br>
When I joined the workstream, the product team was struggling to justify competing screen designs. Screens were built based on stakeholder assumptions, not verified user needs, and neither side could clearly articulate why their design was best.

I conducted 12 user interviews and identified that a critical column, essential to efficiently processing submissions, was missing from the Manager View. This missing data led to delays and unnecessary back-and-forth communication between manufacturers and the FDA.

Teams previously assumed the “Coordinator View” was needed, but I discovered its only real value was that it included this missing data. I recommended removing the redundant Coordinator View and adding the key column to the Manager View. This change streamlined workflows, enabling device manufacturers to respond to FDA feedback more quickly and effectively.

**The full story:** <br>
When I joined the FDA CDRH program as a UX Design Lead, everything about the role felt new. New team. New consulting environment. New expectations. What wasn’t new was the warning I heard almost immediately:<br><br>

“You’ve been assigned to the hardest client here.”<br><br>

People didn’t say it outright. They whispered it in side chats. They hesitated before saying the client’s name. And once, after I asked a clarifying question in a meeting, a Business Analyst messaged me privately:<br><br>

“Let me do the talking.”<br><br>

That was the moment I realized the real challenge wasn’t the interface. It was fear.

The product itself was a customer collaboration portal used by medical device manufacturers—people building tools that save lives. The team was stuck debating competing screen designs, especially around different dashboard views. Each version had champions. No one could clearly articulate why one was better. Decisions were driven by stakeholder assumptions instead of verified user needs.

That became the first mystery: If everyone is so confident in their designs, why can’t anyone explain what problem they solve?

And then the second question followed: If no one feels safe pushing back, how do we figure out what users actually need?

I didn’t want fear deciding what 100,000 manufacturers experienced, so I went to the source.<br><br>

**Discovery: Qualitative research to surface the real workflow**<br>

I conducted 12 in-depth interviews with device manufacturers across company size, submission volume, and regulatory familiarity. These were not usability tests yet. They were workflow conversations.

I asked them to walk me through:

- How submissions are processed;

- Where decisions slow down;

- What information they'd scan first;

- What triggers follow-up questions from the FDA;

- Where frustration quietly accumulates.

As I synthesized the interviews using affinity mapping, a pattern emerged that no one on the product team had noticed because no one had argued about it.

Manufacturers weren’t confused by the number of screens.
They were slowed down by missing information at the moment decisions were made.<br><br>

**Definition: Quantitative signals that confirmed the story**<br>

To validate what I was hearing, I reviewed usage data and workflow artifacts tied to submission processing and follow-up cycles. While I didn’t need heavy analytics to see the problem, the numbers reinforced the interviews:

- Repeated back-and-forth communications;

- Delays in responding to FDA feedback;

- Frequent context switching between views;

- Unnecessary handoffs between roles.

This is when the real mystery cracked open.

The team had long assumed the Coordinator View was essential.
But when I mapped interview insights against the screens, I saw something unexpected:

The only reason users valued the Coordinator View was that it contained one critical data column that was missing from the Manager View.

That missing column was forcing manufacturers to leave their primary workflow just to get basic context.

So the problem wasn’t “Which view is better?”
The problem was why essential information was hidden at all.<br><br>

**Design: Turning insight into a clear, defensible recommendation**<br>

Instead of debating screens, I reframed the conversation around information architecture and decision-making moments.

I produced a research-backed recommendation that was intentionally calm and precise:

- Remove the redundant Coordinator View;

- Add the missing column to the Manager View;

- Align the dashboard with how manufacturers actually process submissions.

I supported the recommendation with:

- Direct interview quotes

- Workflow diagrams

- Before-and-after task flows

- Evidence showing reduced cognitive load and fewer handoffs

And _that’s_ when the tension peaked.

Teammates asked me quietly, “Do you really want to say this?” Others suggested softening the findings.

But the research was clear.
And more importantly, it was accurate.

I didn’t argue.
I let the users speak.<br><br>

**Validation: When evidence shifted the room**<br>

When we presented the findings, the Zoom room felt tight.
Then something unexpected happened.

The client didn’t resist.<br>
They agreed.<br>
Then they endorsed the change.<br>

By focusing on why the Coordinator View existed rather than defending it, the conversation shifted from opinions to outcomes. The redesigned Manager View reduced delays, cut unnecessary communication loops, and helped manufacturers respond to FDA feedback more efficiently.

Today, those changes support over 100,000 device manufacturers, improving collaboration between industry professionals and FDA reviewers and helping life-saving devices reach the market faster.<br><br>

**✨ Lesson: Quiet courage can change systems**<br>

This project taught me that leadership in UX doesn’t always look like being the loudest voice in the room.

Sometimes it looks like:

- Asking the question no one wants to ask;

- Trusting research over assumptions;

- Letting users speak when people are afraid to;

- Holding steady when it would be easier to soften the truth.

A well-researched user story can shift even the most cautious room.
And sometimes the most impactful design change isn’t a new screen. It’s the courage to move one missing piece into the right place.
