**Project:** Website Redesign for Texas Legislative Council <br>
**Client:** Texas Legislative Council, Research Division <br>
**Role:** Solo UX Designer & Project Manager

**The tldr;** <br>
I spearheaded the redesign of 4 external agency applications over several years, operating as the sole UX professional on the team. Before the redesign, user engagement was low and stakeholder feedback indicated difficulty finding key resources.

To understand user needs, I conducted user interviews and usability tests, uncovering that our navigation structure and content hierarchy were confusing and unaligned with how real users worked. I simplified screens and reorganized content by priority, leading to cleaner, more intuitive navigation.

As a result, we saw a 54% increase in time spent on key pages and achieved a 90% customer satisfaction rating post-launch. Following the redesign, the council reported improved access for external users seeking legislative guidance and updates.
<br>
<br>
**The full story:** <br>
When I accepted a Web Designer role at the Texas Legislative Council, I thought I’d be improving a website.

What I didn’t realize was that I was walking into a place where I would have to teach an entire institution what UX even was, and why it mattered.

At first, it felt like I had accidentally joined two different agencies under the same roof. In one division, user research was not only respected, but it was mandatory. In mine, research was dismissed outright. UX was treated like a novelty at best, and a distraction at worst. Senior leadership regularly blocked the very research I was hired to do. I heard “no” so often it began to feel procedural.

That became the first mystery that pulled me in: If UX clearly works elsewhere in the organization, why is it being rejected here?

And then a harder question followed: If I don’t have permission, authority, or precedent, can I still practice real UX?

Something in me refused to let that go.
Because the people impacted by our work weren’t abstract “users.” They were citizens trying to understand the laws that governed their lives. If anyone deserved clarity, it was them.

So I stopped waiting for approval and started working with what I had.<br><br>

**Discovery: Learning to research without asking for permission**<br>

Since formal research approvals were rarely granted, I adapted. I embedded lightweight qualitative research into everyday work:

- Informal intercept conversations framed as “public service feedback”;

- Observational research while citizens navigated legislative resources;

- Pattern tracking from help desk questions and email inquiries.

I listened closely, documented everything, and built credibility quietly. I synthesized insights using simple affinity mapping and shared them in plain language, avoiding UX jargon that triggered resistance.

I wasn’t calling it “research.”
I was calling it “listening.”<br><br>

**Definition: Making evidence visible in a resistant system**<br>

Without access to analytics tools at first, I used proxy quantitative signals:

- Repeat page visits;

- Frequent search terms;

- Common failure paths reported by staff;

- Time-based spikes during legislative sessions.

Over time, I paired these signals with qualitative findings to frame problems not as design opinions, but as service gaps. I translated UX insights into outcomes leadership already cared about: clarity, efficiency, and public trust.

Slowly, the conversation shifted from “Why are we doing this?” to “What are you seeing?”<br><br>

**Design: Building systems, not just screens**<br>

Knowing I wouldn’t always be there, I focused on sustainability. I created the agency’s first design documentation so decisions didn’t disappear when people moved on. I trained colleagues in basic research methods so UX wouldn’t live with me alone.

To lower resistance, I restructured team meetings into feedback sessions. Instead of asking people to critique abstract ideas, I presented vetted options and invited democratic voting. People felt heard. And when people felt heard, they stopped blocking progress.

That was another quiet lesson: sometimes change isn’t about persuasion.
It’s about participation.<br><br>

**Validation: The day “impossible” research actually worked**<br>

There’s one moment I’ll never forget.

I needed to recruit public users during an active legislative session—peak chaos. I was told flat out that it couldn’t be done. Too risky. Too busy. Too visible.

But instead of framing the ask as usability testing, I reframed it as citizen service.
We weren’t testing a site.
We were making sure Texans could access the laws that affected them.

That single shift in language changed perceptions.

We got approval.
We recruited real users.
And the insights we gathered directly shaped improvements that made the site clearer and more navigable during the most critical moments of public engagement.<br><br>

**✨ Lesson: UX leadership isn’t always invited**<br>

Looking back, I didn’t realize how much I was learning while I was just trying to get the work done.

I learned how to:

- Lead without authority;

- Practice research without formal approval;

- Navigate resistance with empathy instead of force;

- Build systems that outlast a single role.

But I also learned something deeper.

This was the moment I discovered my vocation.
I fell in love with helping people make sense of public systems, especially the ones that feel intimidating, opaque, or exclusionary. That job didn’t just make me a UX practitioner. It made me an advocate for human-centered public service.

And once you learn that kind of work is possible, you can’t unsee it.
